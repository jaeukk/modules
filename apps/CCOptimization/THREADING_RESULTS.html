CCOptimization: scheduling and L-BFGS history

October 19, 2026. Measurements use implementation commit 856d6d104dd8a114a63051f61a500899d53e5ac2; the subsequent default change selects history 40. Interactive report.

Selected settings

Use Option A, L-BFGS history 40, and relative contact strength 100. Option A already was master's scheduler and remains the default. FINUFFT remains opt-in through finufft. The shared C++ RelaxStructure_NLOPT history default changes from 100 to 40; history 100 in GetMultiCCO restores the former setting. The required contact-strength input must explicitly be 100. The potential still supports other explicitly requested strengths; no formula or input format changes.

GetCCO and GetMultiCCO run C++ NLopt L-BFGS. Python in the comparison launches processes and analyzes results; it does not optimize configurations. The old Della paper adapter separately uses SciPy L-BFGS-B with history 20. Updating its linked evaluator does not change its optimizer or history. The measurements here do not establish the performance of that SciPy workflow. New native-C++ sweeps require an explicit protocol record and separate provenance from the old campaign.

What the schedules do

A: an outer OpenMP pool schedules independent species density work, eligible force transforms, and contact chunks. Each FINUFFT plan has one internal thread. The existing particle partition and force cost gate remain in effect. Eligible Type 2 transforms batch spatial force components. Species count plus one is a reasonable initial allocation, not a guarantee of full utilization.

B: complete each species' density and forces sequentially. Each FINUFFT plan requests the entire outer thread budget. An additional asynchronous contact worker time-shares the same allocated CPUs. Four allocated CPUs therefore permit four internal FINUFFT threads plus one runnable contact worker, not a 3+1 split. Type 2 transforms batch spatial components without A's concurrency cost gate. Select this optional schedule with finufft-sequential; finufft switches back to A. Compatible plans persist and moving particle positions are refreshed with setpts. Neither schedule requires a different optimizer.

The implementation adds exception-safe contact joining, thread restoration, direct fallbacks, CLI selection, focused tests, and reproducible comparison tools. History input now rejects negative, fractional, malformed, and overflowing values before passing a correction-pair count to NLopt. The candidate energy in the optional MINOP optimizer is initialized within each iteration, removing a GCC 11 uninitialized-value diagnostic without changing the line search or the selected NLopt workflow. It does not migrate MSPB or SpherePack into core or alter scientific formulas, the three NLopt scaling phases, acceptance criteria, or storage formats.

All initial and final energy/force checks passed against direct sums. None of these 24 bounded optimization runs reached the ground-state threshold. Their times are not time to a ground state. Different evaluation counts and trajectories also limit comparisons of complete optimizations.

A is faster at every measured size. Larger transforms reduce B's disadvantage here, but these data establish no crossover at which B becomes preferable. FINUFFT's internal synchronization and small transform sizes are plausible contributors; no profiler isolates their individual time costs. Serial NLopt history and line-search work also limit whole-run utilization. Higher CPU utilization alone is not a performance objective.

An additional 4,000-particles/species bounded replay found byte-identical A endpoints for the repeated seed 17, but different B endpoints between repetitions. At identical coordinates, A/B energy and force differences were at most 1.78e-14 and 2.05e-16, respectively. These endpoints were not ground states. Consequently this test cannot establish that B fails to find or reproduce ground states. Internal floating-point summation order can affect optimizer trajectories; the specific cause of the observed B variation was not isolated.

History 40 reduces median wall time by 48.1% (1.93 times faster). Both histories repeat their own endpoints byte-for-byte for every tested seed. Their endpoints and final energies differ across histories. Every final scaling phase reported NLOPT_FAILURE; these timings describe bounded unsuccessful runs. Reduced history lowers storage and history-update work, but trajectory changes contribute to the timing difference too. This is not a general ground-state success-rate estimate.

Longer strength-100 pilots at phi 0.50, 0.30, and 0.10 also failed to reach the acceptance threshold for seed 17 within 10,000 evaluations per phase. No tolerance was loosened and no failed pilot was classified as an accepted ground state.

Ground-state identity test: a separate strength-1 experiment

The final short identity test used contact strength 1, phi 0.10, Option A, 4,000 particles/species, the same random seed 17, and two runs per history. It used standard C++ NLopt with 10,000 evaluations per phase and no continuation or reference-packing starts. The actual finite-system chi was 0.3005751438.

History Median wall time Evaluations Direct final energy Accepted grounds
100 26.997 s 2,081 1.85e-20 2/2
40 14.554 s 2,152 1.27e-20 2/2

All three NLopt phases returned success. Independent direct Fourier sums and periodic contact checks verified energy <1e-14, constrained species S <1e-10, maximum force component <1e-7, and relative overlap <1e-6. Across all four endpoints, maximum energy was 1.85e-20, maximum constrained S was 1.44e-20, maximum force component was 1.03e-12, and overlap was exactly zero.

Each history repeats its own endpoint byte-for-byte. The two histories produce distinct valid ground states: labeled periodic RMS displacement after translation alignment is 1.99 diameters, and translation/permutation/square-symmetry invariant structure-factor probes also differ. Identical coordinates are not required for finding degenerate ground states.

The zero-overlap endpoints also pass the same physical checks when independently reevaluated at strength 100. They were optimized at strength 1. This does not demonstrate strength-100 convergence or a 1.85-times ground-state speedup at strength 100. Only one random seed was tested. Production remains at strength 100.

Evidence, reproduction, and verification

Compact raw measurements, summaries, build hashes, environment, and the history capture harnesses are stored under tests/threading/results-20261019/. Full historical MSPB snapshots and detailed local logs remain in the original /tmp/cco-*20261019 artifact directories; they are not committed as research data. Those local paths are temporary. The committed JSON preserves measured outcomes and endpoint hashes, but independent coordinate reanalysis requires the snapshots or a rerun. The report does not silently substitute a new build for the measured one.

The scheduling implementation received two Claude Opus audit cycles before commit 856d6d1. Focused tests cover direct references, finite differences, persistent plans, motion, batching, thread limits, fallbacks, toggling, and concurrent-contact exceptions. Release validation also checks the default/explicit history-40 endpoint identity, history-100 override, CLI export/resume, MSPB archive compatibility, and offline PairStat. See threading notes for commands.

No Della speedup or full CPU utilization is guaranteed by these local results. Validate the native execution path and effective settings on a Genoa compute node before production; keep releases immutable and skip previously explored sweep points using receipts rather than only accepted-state counts.
